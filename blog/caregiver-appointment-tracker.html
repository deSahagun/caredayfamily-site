How to keep track of an aging parent's doctor appointments

By Alex, founder of CareDay Family

If you help a parent with their health, you've probably become their appointment manager too: the cardiologist in March, the eye doctor in May, lab work before the next checkup. You're far from alone. 63 million Americans, nearly 1 in 4 adults, are family caregivers, up 20 million in ten years. Six in ten of them are also holding down a job.1

And the doctors add up fast. In a large study of Medicare patients, the typical person saw two primary care doctors and five specialists, spread across four different practices, in a single year.2 Each office has its own phone number, its own portal and its own reminders, and most of those reminders go to your parent, not to you.

You don't need a better memory. You need a caregiver appointment tracker you can actually keep up with. Here's a simple system.

1. Make one list of every doctor

Write down each doctor's name, specialty, office address, phone number and patient portal. Keep it on your phone, not on a sheet of paper in your parent's kitchen drawer. The first time an office calls to reschedule, you'll know exactly who it is.

2. Put every appointment in one place, the day it's booked

Appointments scattered across a parent's calendar, a sticky note and a voicemail are the ones that get missed. Use one family medical calendar for everyone you look after: your parent, your kids and yourself. Give each person their own color so a quick glance tells you whose day it is.

3. Set reminders for you, not just for them

Clinic reminders go to the patient. As the caregiver, you need your own: a week ahead to arrange time off or a ride, a day ahead to confirm, and an hour ahead to get out the door. A medical appointment tracker that reminds you on that schedule takes the "did I forget something?" feeling off your plate.

4. Keep the essentials with you

Every visit needs the same few things: the insurance card (front and back), a current medication list and a short list of questions. Take photos of the cards and keep them on your phone, so they're there in the waiting room even when the wallet isn't.

5. Share the load with siblings

If brothers or sisters help out, whoever is driving needs the time and the address, not a vague "Mom has the cardiologist Tuesday." Send the full details by text the moment an appointment is booked. A shared message beats "I thought you were taking her."

6. After every visit, write down what's next

Follow-up in three months? New blood work? A referral to another specialist? Add it before you leave the parking lot, while it's fresh. That one habit keeps the next appointment from slipping through the cracks.
